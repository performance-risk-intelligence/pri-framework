01 / THE PROBLEM & THE VALUE

You have the signals.
Can you act on them?

For teams facing inconsistent benchmarks, repeat regressions, or unclear release ownership, PRI proposes a connected way to measure, decide, and learn. Start with the gap that matters to your team.

01 / MEASUREMENT

“Are these results really comparable?”

Different workloads, environments, or timing boundaries can make a release comparison difficult to trust.

What you would receive
A defined user journey, benchmark procedure, workload conditions, and baseline report with uncertainty and failed outcomes recorded.
Value to evaluate
More repeatable comparisons. Track run-to-run variation, measurement coverage, and evidence completeness.
02 / RELEASE DECISIONS

“Who decides when a change is ready?”

A dashboard can show a change without establishing who reviews it, what evidence is sufficient, or how an exception is approved.

What you would receive
Proposed risk criteria, validation thresholds, a decision owner, and a release-readiness checklist with exception records.
Value to evaluate
Traceable decisions. Track review coverage, unresolved evidence gaps, and documented high-risk exceptions.
03 / REPEAT INCIDENTS

“Why are we fixing this again?”

Incident action items can lose their connection to future tests and release decisions.

What you would receive
An incident-linked risk register and a process for turning findings into owned tests, controls, or instrumentation changes.
Value to evaluate
Stronger follow-through. Track preventive-action completion, control effectiveness, and recurrence over a meaningful period.
04 / RESOURCE EFFICIENCY

“Did this release make each job cost more?”

Retries and longer processing can increase resource use, while demand and pricing changes make attribution harder.

What you would receive
A measurement plan linking comparable workloads, completion, retries, compute use, and attributable cost to release changes.
Value to evaluate
Better visibility into avoidable resource use. Track compute and cost per completed action alongside reliability.

These are proposed deliverables and intended benefits to evaluate, not achieved PRI customer outcomes. An assessment would first determine which gaps exist and whether PRI is a useful fit.

Who would use it?

Engineering and platform leaders, SRE teams, performance engineers, FinOps professionals, and technology-risk stakeholders. Potential settings include SaaS, AI-enabled services, fintech, healthcare technology, and cloud-backed products. Suitability depends on the workflow, available evidence, and team capacity—not company size alone.

A governance layer that works with your tools.

PRI is designed to complement benchmarks, observability, CI/CD, and incident-management practices. It does not replace them or guarantee that a release is safe.

How the evidence becomes a decision

Begin with measurement and explicit rules. Predictive methods would be introduced only after sufficient authorized data and evaluation support their use.

1. Define a journey and establish comparable evidence

A “golden journey” is an important user action with agreed start and end points. Define representative workloads, environment and version identifiers, repeat runs, acceptable measurement variation, and missing-data rules. Record latency distributions alongside completion, failure, and retry rates so unsuccessful interactions do not disappear from the comparison.

Planned measurement artifacts include a journey catalog, benchmark procedure, instrumentation naming and segmentation standards, and reports showing differences between releases and confidence limitations. Real-user telemetry would supplement controlled tests where authorized and available.

2. Connect changes and incident history to release risk

Review benchmark changes with relevant release context: dependency changes, feature flags, infrastructure changes, and known incidents. Initially, documented criteria can identify where more validation is needed. The predictive pillar would later require a labeled regression dataset, evaluated scoring methods, an explanation of contributing signals, and governed thresholds.

No validated predictive model is provided in this edition. A risk score would support review; it would not replace evidence or an authorized release decision.

3. Assign controls, owners, and exception requirements

Link identified risks to tests, gates, alerts, or runbooks. Define who reviews a threshold breach, what mitigation is required, and when a change needs escalation. An exception record should identify the decision-maker, supporting evidence, rationale, residual risk, follow-up owner, and expiry or review condition.

A review record would preserve the release identifier, measurements, decision, and evidence links. Thresholds and authority must fit the adopting organization’s own processes.

4. Turn incidents into prevention and evaluate the controls

Use incident findings to select a durable preventive action: a new benchmark, regression test, gate, instrumentation change, or revised runbook. Assign an owner, due date, and verification step, then watch for recurrence.

Compare outcomes over an appropriate period and record workload or architecture changes that could affect the result. Fewer incidents or lower costs alone would not establish that PRI caused the change.

04 / A FOCUSED START

Define one workflow.
Assess readiness.

A proposed PRI readiness assessment begins with a bounded journey and an agreed question. Scope and evaluation criteria depend on the environment and available evidence.

  1. 01

    Frame the journey

    Agree on start and end points, workload conditions, business relevance, and authorized data.

  2. 02

    Establish the baseline

    Measure time, completion, failures, and retries. Record uncertainty and incomplete outcomes.

  3. 03

    Connect risk to controls

    Review changes and available incident history. Propose tests, thresholds, ownership, and exceptions.

  4. 04

    Evaluate the next step

    Review practical value, implementation dependencies, and whether a separately scoped pilot makes sense.

PROPOSED OUTPUTS

Baseline report / Incident-linked risk register / Release-risk criteria / Regression-test plan / Readiness checklist

This describes a proposed service model, not an offer of an immediately available production product. Commercial assessments and pilots are planned for the commercial dissemination stage, following framework development and subject to separate agreement.

What your team would contribute

A workflow owner, representative scenarios, and agreed access to relevant benchmark, release, incident, and cost records where available. Discovery would identify missing inputs, confidentiality requirements, and implementation effort before scope is agreed.

What happens after the assessment?

A separately agreed pilot could implement selected controls within one team or pipeline. Expansion would depend on practical value, evidence quality, and team capacity. Proposed training and handover materials would help the team maintain the controls.

05 / POTENTIAL BROADER VALUE

From one workflow
to shared capability.

PRI’s potential U.S. economic value would depend on adoption and demonstrated improvements within organizations. Reusable methods and training could extend useful practices beyond a single engagement.

More dependable digital services

If controls reduce the frequency or duration of regressions, organizations could experience fewer disrupted transactions and incomplete user journeys. Evidence would require comparable completion, timeliness, and incident records.

Engineering time for productive work

If recurring problems become less frequent, teams could spend less time on repeated diagnosis and emergency remediation. Track actual incident effort and the cost of maintaining controls to assess net value.

More efficient infrastructure use

Reducing unnecessary retries or processing could lower resource use per completed action. Billing effects depend on pricing and capacity; electricity effects require workload and facility data or a clearly qualified model.

EXTERNAL CONTEXT · NOT A PRI IMPACT ESTIMATE

Berkeley Lab’s United States Data Center Energy Usage Report: 2025 Update, published in June 2026, projects that data centers could account for 9.5–15.3% of U.S. electricity use in 2030 across its overall uncertainty range. This is a national infrastructure forecast, not an estimate of waste or the share PRI could affect. Read the report, Executive Summary, printed page 5.

Measure locally before projecting broadly.

No national dollar savings, job-creation totals, or PRI energy-reduction estimates are asserted here. A defensible projection would require validated outcomes, adoption evidence, costs, uncertainty ranges, and controls against double counting. Read the value-measurement approach and sources.
